# Chapter 25: The Monk

![](images/image-1.jpg)

(360)
It’s good to guard your eyes.
It’s good to guard your ears.
It’s good to guard your nose and tongue.
Self-control begins at the gate of the senses.

(361)
Control your body.
Control your speech.
Control your thoughts.
Mastery in all three
frees you from suffering.

(362)
He walks gently, speaks carefully,
and keeps to his path.
He reflects deeply, meditates sincerely,
and is content with little.
That’s who people call a monk.

(363)
A true monk is not just quiet—
he speaks with grace,
not too much, never boastful.
And when he shares the Teaching,
his words reflect both truth and spirit.

(364)
A monk who lives in the Dhamma,
finds joy in it,
meditates on it,
and remembers it well—
he won’t fall away.

(365)
Don’t look down on what you’ve been given.
Don’t envy others for what they have.
A monk who clings to comparisons
misses the stillness of the mind.

(366)
But the monk who accepts even little with gratitude,
lives purely, and works hard—
he is praised, even by the gods.

(367)
If you have no attachment to body or mind,
and no sorrow for what you lack—
then you’re truly free.
You’re truly a monk.

(368)
The monk who lives in loving-kindness
and holds the Buddha’s path close to his heart
finds the stillness that ends all becoming.

(369)
Empty your boat, O monk.
Let go of greed.
Let go of hate.
Only then will your boat float lightly
to the far shore.

(370)
Cut off five things.
Leave behind five more.
Grow five new habits.
A monk who crosses the flood
knows what to let go and what to cultivate.

(371)
Meditate. Don’t be careless.
Don’t get drunk on the senses.
If you are careless,
it’s like swallowing a red-hot iron ball—
you’ll scream later, “Why is this burning?!”

(372)
There’s no real meditation without wisdom.
There’s no real wisdom without meditation.
But when both grow together,
you’re close to the end of all suffering.

(373)
The monk who finds solitude,
who calms his mind and sees clearly—
he knows a joy that nothing in this world can match.

(374)
To watch the body rise and fall,
to truly see impermanence—
is to taste the joy of something deeper.
To the wise, this points to the Deathless.

(375)
Discipline in the senses.
Simplicity in living.
Following the monastic path—
this is the ground where peace begins.

(376)
Stay close to friends who are noble and kind,
pure in life and strong in heart.
Live with warmth and good conduct,
and joy will carry you beyond suffering.

(377)
Just as the jasmine sheds old petals,
you must shed greed and hate.
Let them fall away.

(378)
The monk whose body is calm,
whose speech is calm,
whose thoughts are calm—
he has dropped the world’s noise.
He is truly serene.

(379)
Examine yourself.
Question yourself.
The monk who guards his mind
walks through the world in peace.

(380)
You are your own guide.
You are your own protector.
So, train yourself—
like a skilled rider guiding
a noble horse.

(381)
Full of joy, full of trust
in the path of the Buddha,
the monk steps quietly
into the bliss of release.

(382)
A young monk who lives the Teaching
is like the moon breaking free from clouds—
he shines on the world.



This copy of the Dhammapada is paraphrased and modernized from the translation by Acharya Buddharakkhita. (Originally published by Buddha Dharma Education Association Inc).
